# Design note — ADI CN0549 reference instrument

Companion to [`hardware/cn0549/README.md`](../../hardware/cn0549/README.md).
The README says what to buy and how to bring it up; this records why the kit is
shaped this way, which of its design assumptions were wrong, and what is open.

---

## 1. The brief

Seven of the eight kits answer "can this silicon do condition monitoring?" This
one answers a different question:

> When a €90 battery MEMS node reports 2.1 mm/s, how does anybody know?

Every other kit's numbers rest on a datasheet. This kit's rest on a certificate:
a calibrated IEPE piezo, measured on a reference shaker, with a serial number
and a date. Its role in the programme is **metrological**, not operational — it
is the instrument the fleet is compared against, deployed in ones rather than
hundreds.

That framing decides everything else. It is mains-powered because it can be; it
samples at 32 kHz with 32768-sample blocks because the only cost is time; its
interval is 15 minutes rather than an hour because it is on a rig, not a
pipeline.

## 2. Two design assumptions, both wrong

### 2.1 "24 bits sees fainter faults"

The purchase argument for a 24-bit converter over a 16-bit MEMS part is that it
resolves 100× finer. It does — and it does not matter, because the measurement
is sensor-limited by two to three orders of magnitude:

| chain | ADC noise density | sensor noise density | sensor/ADC |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| IIS3DWB 16-bit ±16 g | 1.11 µg/√Hz | 75 µg/√Hz | 67× |
| ADXL355 20-bit ±8 g | 0.03 µg/√Hz | 25 µg/√Hz | 718× |
| CN0549 24-bit ±41 g | 0.01 µg/√Hz | 7 µg/√Hz | 628× |

(ADC noise is LSB/√12 spread over fs/2; sensor noise is the datasheet density.)

The first version of `experiments.py` quantised one record to all three depths
and compared the BPFO harmonic ratio. The three results agreed to three decimal
places at every severity. A less careful reading of that would have been "the
experiment is broken"; it was the answer.

So the experiment was rewritten around noise density — and **that** was also not
what the design assumed. All three chains first separate fault from healthy at
simulated severity 0.01. A 10× quieter sensor gives a *stronger* signature once
one exists (3.26 vs 2.00 at severity 0.01, which helps severity estimation) but
does not detect earlier, because the machine's own broadband noise, not the
sensor's, sets the floor.

**What survives as a justification**, and it is enough:

1. **Bandwidth, DC–40 kHz.** Categorical, not incremental: lubrication distress
   and early crack propagation emit at 20–40 kHz, and no MEMS part in this
   lineup can see that band at all. The IIS3DWB stops at 6.3 kHz.
2. **Traceability.** When a fleet of MEMS nodes disagrees, a certificate settles
   it. Nothing else here can.
3. **DC accuracy.** DC-coupled through the converter, so tilt and thrust are
   measurements rather than artefacts of an AC-coupling corner.

The honest conclusion is that this is a **bandwidth-and-traceability** purchase
wearing a resolution label.

### 2.2 "A disconnected sensor is invisible"

The prediction: converter noise with no sensor is the smoothest signal the
system will ever see, so a one-class detector — which measures distance from
healthy — would score it *below* healthy, and the worst possible condition would
be invisible.

Measured, with a Mahalanobis detector calibrated to 1 % FPR:

| | median score | alarms |
|---|---:|---:|
| threshold | 9.7 | — |
| healthy | 5.8 | 0/20 |
| disconnected | 52.9 | 20/20 |
| outer-race 0.35 | 84.8 | 20/20 |

Wrong, and the reason is worth internalising: *distance from healthy is not
ordered by smoothness*. Converter noise is far outside the healthy manifold, in
a different direction from a fault, and a quadratic-form detector does not care
which direction. Featureless is as anomalous as faulty.

The real failure is a level up, and it is worse than the prediction because it
reaches a human. The detector alarms, and the rule base diagnoses the noise:

```
disconnected -> mechanical_looseness   confidence 0.97  severity developing
```

A cut cable produces a **97 %-confidence looseness work order**. A fitter is
dispatched to a healthy machine; the real fault is a connector; and when the
fitter finds nothing, the programme loses credibility in exactly the way that
ends programmes.

This cannot be fixed in the data — both cases are "far from healthy, in a
direction the rules have an explanation for". It needs an out-of-band check,
which is what §3 is.

## 3. The IEPE bias check, and why it refuses

An IEPE sensor idles at a known bias on its constant-current supply, so the
bias voltage *is* the cable's state: at the supply rail, open; near zero,
shorted; in band, live.

`CN0549Source.open()` raises rather than warning. The reasoning is asymmetric
cost: a node that refuses to start produces a loud, immediate, correctly-
attributed failure, while a node that starts on a cut cable produces months of
data showing a beautifully smooth machine — and **nobody questions good news**.
The second failure is silent, long-lived and ends in a surprise breakdown.

`unknown` — between a short and the normal window — is also not a pass. That
band is where a wrong sensor type, an over-long cable and a failing current
source live, and "probably fine" is how §2.2 begins.

`classify_bias` is a pure function taking a voltage and a supply rail, so the
whole decision table is tested with no hardware. That matters more than usual
here: this is the check that catches the failure the data cannot show, so it is
the last thing that should be untested.

**Open item:** the bias should be *trended*, not just gated. A slowly falling
bias is a failing current source or a wetting connector, visible weeks before it
trips the threshold. The same applies to the DC offset, which tracks the current
source and the sensor's temperature.

## 4. The filter is a choice, and DC removal has an order

### 4.1 sinc5 vs wideband

The AD7768-1's two decimation paths trade transition sharpness against group
delay. `sinc5` is sharp with an impulse response spanning tens of samples;
`wideband` is flat to 0.433 × Nyquist with a short response.

For a magnitude spectrum, sinc5. For **impact timing and envelope shape** —
which is what bearing diagnosis is — wideband, because sinc5's long response
smears the impacts the envelope detector exists to find.

Neither is safe as a default, so `filter_type` is a required constructor
argument with no default value and the agent prints it at start-up. A filter
choice that silently changes what the numbers mean must not be invisible.

### 4.2 Clip check before DC removal

This chain is DC-coupled, so the bias appears as a large DC offset, which must
be removed before any spectral work: left in, it puts a huge value in bin 0 that
leaks into the low orders through any window's skirts.

The removal happens **after** the clipping check, and the order is the whole
point. The converter clips on the *total* signal including the offset. Removing
the offset first and then checking for clipping would hide exactly the condition
being looked for — a signal riding a large offset that saturates on one side
only.

The removed amount is reported rather than discarded, for the trending reason in
§3.

## 5. What a review should attack first

1. **The IIO attribute names.** `filter_type`, `sensor_bias_voltage`,
   `sample_rate` — these differ between pyadi-iio versions and between the
   AD7768-1 and AD7768 drivers. Nothing here has run against a device.
   `read_bias_v` tries three names and returns NaN rather than guessing, which
   `classify_bias` reports as `unknown`, so the failure mode is safe — but it
   means the check could be silently unavailable.
2. **The simulator's noise model.** Both experiments rest on it. It was written
   in this repository, by the same process that wrote the features, so some of
   the agreement is self-fulfilling. §2.1's *physics* argument (sensor noise
   dominates ADC noise by 600×) is datasheet arithmetic and stands on its own;
   the *detection threshold* result does not.
3. **The 20–40 kHz claim.** The kit's strongest remaining justification is a
   band nothing here has measured. It is plausible on the AE literature and it
   is untested.
4. **The IEPE pulse budget.** 4 mA at 24 V is 96 mW continuous just to bias the
   sensor. On a 2600 mAh 18650 that is ~11 days. Stated so nobody proposes a
   battery version.
5. **SD-card wear.** `synchronous=FULL` is one fsync per frame. At 15-minute
   cadence that is 96 writes/day and irrelevant; at the 1 Hz an instrument might
   be asked for, it is not, and the carrier would need eMMC.

## 6. Open items, ordered by what they would change

| Item | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Cross-validate against a MEMS node on one housing** | Replaces §2.1's simulator result with a real one. The highest-value missing experiment anywhere in this repository |
| Reference-shaker calibration | Without it the kit is an expensive MEMS node: the certificate *is* the product |
| Measure the 20–40 kHz band under lubrication starvation | Tests the kit's best categorical argument |
| Verify the bias check on hardware | Unplug mid-run; §2.2 predicts 97 % looseness if the check is bypassed |
| Trend the bias and the DC offset | Weeks of warning on a failing current source |
| Pin the Kuiper image | Reproducibility; an unpinned `apt` source is a silent dependency change |
| UPS or holdover | A power cut loses the cycle in progress. The queue survives; the acquisition does not |

---

## 7. What this kit is for, in one paragraph

One or two per programme, on a rig or on the most critical asset, as the
instrument everything else is compared against. It is mains-powered by
necessity — IEPE excitation makes low power impossible by design — costs around
€700 against the STWIN's €90, and its advantage is **not** the resolution its
datasheet sells: it is 40 kHz of bandwidth no MEMS part here can reach, and a
calibration certificate that makes its numbers mean something. Buy it to
validate a fleet, not to be one.
